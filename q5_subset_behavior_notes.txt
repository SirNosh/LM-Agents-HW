Question 5 — What the actual 1,000-conversation training subset teaches
=======================================================================

Source: data/ultrachat_train_1000_seed42.jsonl (all 1,000 saved rows inspected
programmatically; examples below refer to 1-based row numbers). This is not a
survey of the full UltraChat dataset or a quantitative test_sft evaluation.

The saved set contains 1,000 alternating user/assistant multi-turn conversations
(3,145 assistant turns). Common examples include:

1. Actionable, structured instructions. Row 1 asks for a customer-feedback
   tracking system; its first assistant answer enumerates collection,
   categorization, response assignment, analysis, remediation, customer
   communication, and monitoring. Row 2 asks for a home-cleaning routine and
   receives a numbered daily/weekly/monthly procedure. Row 1 later asks for
   more detail on root-cause remediation; row 2 later asks specifically about
   organizing a home office. The desired behavior is to answer with concrete,
   organized steps and adapt the details to the user's setting.

2. Conversational follow-up and revision. Row 20 starts with a short story and
   later asks for more dialogue between the characters; the assistant revises
   the story. Row 551 asks for a Python rectangle calculator and later asks for
   comments on each step; the assistant returns a commented version. The
   desired behavior is to use prior turns, acknowledge a refinement, and
   revise the answer rather than restart or ignore the new constraint.

3. Grounded question answering. Row 10 asks how long fig jam can be stored,
   providing recipe text, and receives a short answer (up to 3 months). Row 3
   asks about a test-taking article, then asks for more tips mentioned there.
   The desired behavior is to extract the answer from supplied text rather
   than invent facts that contradict it.

4. Creative generation and elaboration. Rows 4, 17, 20, and 801 ask for
   stories or imaginative world-building and then ask for elaboration. This
   pattern encourages a conversational, detailed writing style, though a
   three-prompt qualitative comparison cannot measure creative quality.

The fixed subset is heterogeneous; it does not teach one narrow skill. It
contains both longer formatted advice and brief factual responses. The
training script uses only assistant-token loss and retains at most the final
512 tokens per conversation, so later follow-up turns can dominate what is
actually optimized. These are intended patterns, not evidence that training
successfully distilled them.

Qualitative comparison
----------------------
q5_qualitative.py uses three NEW prompts (tool-library plan, clothing-swap
follow-up with accessibility/time constraints, and a repair-cafe notice) for
the base model, LoRA ranks 1/4/16, and full fine-tuning. These are not copied
from the saved training rows and are not sampled from test_sft. Outputs are in
results/sft_lora_20260924_204124/qualitative_comparison.txt.

Observed differences across models:

* On the tool-library plan, the base answer is relatively concrete (it asks for
  the tool, damage date, and location) but contradicts itself about what to tell
  borrowers when a tool is unrepairable. LoRA r=1 gives a cleaner library-system
  and borrower-notification sequence, although it trails off. r=4 suggests a
  notebook/camera and repair service, including potentially invented app names.
  r=16 and full FT give more steps, but also add redundant or nonsensical
  advice: r=16 repeats the impossible "replace or repair" instruction and adds
  cleanup/disposal/record-keeping; full FT says to repair a tool "if [it] is not
  damaged" and adds training that was not requested.
* On the clothing-swap follow-up, the base largely ignores the two-hour setup
  constraint and produces malformed repeated chat-template text. r=1 remembers
  the prior plan but drifts into repeated donate/recycle steps and does not
  address wheelchair access. r=4 and r=16 explicitly add accessibility features
  (ramps/elevators) and keep the clothing-swap context, but neither gives a
  practical two-hour schedule; r=16's answer is cut off. Full FT mentions the
  two-hour constraint but only repeats it as a planning item rather than
  breaking setup into timed tasks. So higher ranks are not uniformly better:
  full FT is the only one to echo that constraint, while r=4 is more specific
  about how tables and venue should support wheelchair users.
* On the notice, all variants make the same key reasoning mistake: they say a
  booking is needed at 11 a.m., even though walk-ins are allowed until noon,
  and they lead with yes to bringing the lamp despite the ban on electrical
  items. Their failure modes differ: base/full FT are comparatively coherent
  but self-contradict; r=1 degenerates into repeated iOS-version text; r=4 emits
  repeated chat-like fragments and stray Chinese tokens; r=16 loops the same
  wrong claim. This makes the severe grounding error shared, with extra
  repetition/formatting problems in the adapters.

Overall, these examples show stylistic differences, not a clear monotonic gain
from increasing LoRA rank. r=1 sometimes adapts the conversational plan well;
r=4 is more explicit about accessibility; r=16 tends to over-elaborate or loop;
full FT is sometimes more fluent and echoes a new constraint, but still makes
basic contradictions. The prompts use deterministic greedy decoding with a
220-token cap, and three examples are too few to claim these are stable model
traits. Treat these as illustrative observations, not a benchmark.

The generation limit is 220 new tokens, so some longer answers stop mid-list.
The checkpoint is Qwen2.5-0.5B base rather than an instruction-tuned model;
that and the short, heterogeneous one-epoch training run limit what one can
conclude from a few greedy samples.
